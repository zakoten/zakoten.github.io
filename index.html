This project is under NDA. Beyond the public homepage, I can’t show screens or share detailed figures. What follows is how I worked and what I decided.

The problem

Every ELIS programme ran on its own platform, even though the client companies were often the same. Information got lost between tools, and each platform meant a separate supplier contract. OneSquare had to bring everything into one place.

My role

I decided the flows and the interactions: how each type of user enters the platform, what they can do on their own, and how they move between areas. The visual design was done by our external partner. I worked in a platform team of four, with the partner’s developers and designers, the internal teams of each area being migrated, and the client companies.

How I approached it

OneSquare started as a pilot. We launched with two priority groups, corporate innovation managers and startups, and designed their experience first. Bringing in the other areas was always the goal, but it depended on how the first weeks went.

Once the early results confirmed the direction, I ran workshops with users from the other groups to understand what they needed to reach the same level as the first two. We held at least one workshop per group, plus one internal session with all ELIS areas.

Key decisions

  • A different homepage for each user type. Corporates, startups, students and HR managers each arrive with a different goal, so each gets their own entry point.
  • Self-service actions. Companies can act on their own, and the internal team handles fewer requests.
  • A gamification layer. It helps companies get familiar with the platform. The system is described below.

The gamification system

I wrote the gamification design document for the platform on my own. The goal was to make collaboration visible and rewarding without turning it into a race. Almost all of it is now live.

  • Points record, they don’t drive. Points help people keep track of the positive things they’ve done. They’re not meant to become the reason to do them.
  • Two separate tracks. One measures the impact of collaborations, mapped to the Sustainable Development Goals. The other measures how well someone knows the platform, and stays private to the user.
  • Collaboration is rewarded in its own right. A dedicated score celebrates working together, whatever the size of the project. It doesn’t feed any leaderboard.
  • Leaderboards that don’t punish. There is no single ranking that compares companies with individuals. Each leaderboard is split by role and shows only the top 10. Anyone outside it sees their own score, not their position.
  • Fair across sizes. Level thresholds are scaled so a large company and a single user face a comparable effort to reach the same level.
  • Missions that appear when they’re relevant. Mission packs unlock at the right moment: at first login for onboarding, when someone first contributes to a goal, when they join an initiative, or when they reach a new level.
  • A mastery bar that grows with the product. When a new feature ships, the bar can drop below 100%, showing there’s something new to learn.
  • Reputation built on facts. After a successful collaboration, the parties can connect their profiles and exchange public, structured feedback.

Outcome

  • Released in January 2026 with 3 areas. 5 more followed.
  • 300+ companies onboarded, each with at least one active user.
  • One supplier instead of many.
  • Usage is higher than on the old platforms.
  • In workshops, users described the experience as “intuitive”.
